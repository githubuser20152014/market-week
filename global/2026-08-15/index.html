The Navy Blockaded Hormuz. The S&P Crossed 7,800 Like Nothing Happened.

The week's defining story was the collision between a cooperative inflation print and a bond market that refused to celebrate. July CPI came in at 3.4% YoY with core at 2.5% annually, landing exactly on consensus and cementing the case for a September rate hold after July's FOMC voted 9-3 to stay put, with three regional presidents dissenting in favor of a 25-basis-point hike. That triple dissent, the first in over a decade, signals a Fed that is institutionally fractured on trajectory. The short end rallied on the CPI data. The long end did not.

The 30-year Treasury yield touched its highest level since 2001, closing the week at 5.26%, up 5 basis points. Soft primary dealer demand at the weekly refunding auction drove the move. This is the regime signal of the week: disinflation at the short end is no longer enough to suppress long-duration borrowing costs when the fiscal deficit trajectory remains unresolved. The yield curve is steepening for the wrong reason. Growth is fine; it is the debt stock that is repricing.

Layered on top of the domestic picture was a Middle East escalation that the market is not fully pricing. The U.S. Navy redirected 59 commercial vessels, disabled three, and boarded two in the Strait of Hormuz this week. The Pentagon announced an "indefinite" rotational blockade of Iranian port traffic following drone strikes on two UAE-flagged tankers. The EIA raised its 2026 energy price baseline. WTI crude surged 5.1% on the week to $82.40, even after a massive 17.4 million barrel inventory build temporarily pushed prices back toward $81.84. Hormuz handles roughly 20% of global daily petroleum shipments. If this blockade holds, the inflation convergence story the Fed is banking on faces a direct supply-side threat before Jackson Hole even opens.

What This Means For You

July CPI at 3.4% YoY and core at 2.5% landed on consensus, cementing a September rate hold and triggering a broad equity rally that pushed the S&P 500 above 7,800 for the first time. The clearest beneficiary was IWM, the small-cap ETF, which gained 1.39% as floating-rate debt concerns eased for smaller companies. The most important structural warning of the week had nothing to do with stocks: the 30-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2001 at 5.26%, driven by soft primary dealer demand at the refunding auction, signaling that long-duration Treasuries are repricing fiscal risk regardless of what the Fed does at the short end. The U.S. Navy's indefinite Hormuz blockade, following drone strikes on UAE-flagged tankers, drove WTI crude up 5.1% to $82.40, a direct supply-side inflation threat that could complicate the Fed's convergence path before Jackson Hole on August 27-29. Investors holding TLT face pressure from both ends: the long end is rising on fiscal concerns while the short end has limited room to fall. A globally diversified portfolio with exposure to EWJ benefited from Japan's third consecutive GDP growth quarter, but Hang Seng weakness via EWH reflects Typhoon Dolphin disruptions and the China-WTO trade dispute, both of which remain live risks heading into next week's Chinese activity data.

The One Trade

The Russell 2000 gained 1.39% this week, outperforming the S&P 500 by nearly a full percentage point, as the July CPI print at 3.4% YoY and the flat PPI report locked in a September rate hold and shifted market pricing toward potential easing later in 2026. Small-cap U.S. companies carry proportionally more floating-rate debt than large caps. When rate hike expectations recede, their interest expense outlook improves immediately. That mechanical relationship drove this week's relative outperformance, and the catalyst is not going away.

The FOMC's 9-3 vote to hold in July, with three hawks dissenting in favor of a hike, is now the known tail risk. But both CPI and PPI came in cooperative this week. The Fed's next meeting is in September, and the data flow between now and then, including the PCE components and Jackson Hole guidance from Chair Warsh on August 27-29, will determine whether the hold becomes a cut. Either outcome is constructive for small caps. A hold removes the rate hike premium. A cut is direct fuel. Hedge funds rebuilt long U.S. equity exposure this week ahead of Jackson Hole, and small caps are the highest-beta expression of that positioning shift in a low-VIX (14.25) environment where investors are rewarded for leaning into the setup.

Confirms: Russell 2000 closes above 3,075 with VIX remaining below 16 into the Jackson Hole week, or any Fed communication explicitly removes September hike risk. Risk: July PCE core inflation comes in above 2.7%, reigniting the three hawkish FOMC dissenters and pushing September rate hike odds above 60%, which reverses the rate-sensitivity tailwind driving this trade.

Equity Markets

U.S. equities delivered a split result. The Russell 2000 gained 1.39% to 3,068, the week's clear domestic outperformer, as cooling CPI data and a reduced probability of further rate hikes benefited rate-sensitive smaller companies. The S&P 500 crossed the historic 7,800 milestone, closing at 7,785.76, up 0.44%, driven by AI infrastructure earnings: Super Micro Computer and CoreWeave each surged 19%, Nvidia added 3%. The Nasdaq gained just 0.18% and the Dow slipped 0.63%, held back by industrial names that do not benefit from the AI capex cycle.

The week's most dramatic equity move came from Japan. The Nikkei 225 jumped 4.51% to 68,713, supported by Japan's third consecutive quarter of GDP growth at 0.6% QoQ (2.3% annualized), with private consumption and capital expenditure both expanding. Eastern Japan flooding during Obon peak travel disrupted some logistics but did not materially dent the index. European equities were quieter. The DAX and Euro Stoxx 50 each added 0.3%, anchored by the Eurozone's own 0.4% Q2 GDP beat. The CAC 40 fell 0.85% and the FTSE 100 dropped 1.39% despite UK GDP also growing 0.4% in Q2, a beat against a consensus expectation of zero growth for June. The FTSE underperformance likely reflects sterling strength and energy sector positioning rather than domestic growth concerns. Hang Seng fell 2.67%, dragged by Typhoon Dolphin disruptions to Eastern Chinese manufacturing and the ongoing WTO dispute filing by Beijing against U.S. industrial tariffs.

Currency Markets

The dollar was effectively unchanged for the week, with the DXY closing at 99.67, up just 0.08%. The dovish CPI read offset any support from rising long yields, leaving the greenback in a holding pattern ahead of Jackson Hole on August 27-29. The most notable currency move was the yen's 0.95% decline against the dollar despite Japan logging its best growth streak in years. Japanese flooding, ongoing global risk appetite, and carry trade dynamics overpowered the macro fundamental case for yen strength. FXY holders took a loss on the week that the GDP data alone would not justify.

Sterling gained 0.35% to 1.3536, the week's best G10 performer, after the UK ONS reported Q2 GDP at 0.4%, with June alone rising 0.3% month-on-month against a consensus of zero. Services sector output rose 0.5% in the quarter, supported by hospitality spending around summer sporting events. The Bank of England's rate-cut path now looks less certain, which supported GBP. The Swiss franc slipped 0.72%, consistent with renewed risk appetite globally. EUR/USD added 0.15% to 1.1573, a muted reaction to the Eurozone's own GDP beat, suggesting the euro's upside is capped by ECB easing expectations that remain more intact than the Fed's.

Commodities & Metals

WTI crude surged 5.08% to $82.40, the week's biggest commodity move and the one with the clearest geopolitical driver. The U.S. Navy's indefinite blockade of Iranian port traffic, drone strikes on UAE-flagged tankers, and the EIA's upward revision to its 2026 price baseline all fed the move. A 17.4 million barrel inventory build mid-week pulled WTI back toward $81.84 before a partial recovery. The inventory data matters: it tells you demand is not what is running this trade. Geopolitical risk premium is doing the work. If the Hormuz standoff de-escalates, the supply premium unwinds fast.

Gold gained 1.02% to close at $4,380, a solid but unspectacular move given the inflationary headline noise and long-end Treasury pressure. Silver outperformed at +1.71% to $64.99, consistent with industrial demand signals from the AI infrastructure build-out and China's NEV sector, where battery electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles captured over 60% of domestic passenger car sales in July for the first time on record. Natural gas added 0.74% to $2.73, a modest move despite record grid load demands across both Southern Europe and Eastern China during concurrent summer heatwaves.

This Week’s Economic Events

The July CPI print was the week's most market-sensitive data release. Headline CPI rose 0.1% MoM and 3.4% YoY; core CPI rose 0.2% MoM and 2.5% YoY. Both figures landed on consensus, delivering maximum policy clarity: a September rate hold is now the base case, with CME FedWatch pricing a near 50-50 split between hold and cut. The flat July PPI (0.0% MoM versus a 0.2% consensus expectation) saw its annual rate decelerate from 5.5% to 4.7%, reinforcing the disinflation signal at the wholesale level, though core PPI ticked up 0.4% due to portfolio management fees, a category that tells you little about goods supply chains.

Outside the U.S., the data week was unusually productive. The Eurozone confirmed Q2 GDP at 0.4%, Japan posted 0.6% QoQ growth for a third consecutive quarter, and the UK beat decisively with 0.4% Q2 GDP and a June reading of 0.3% against a zero consensus. These three prints together describe a global economy that is growing modestly and broadly, not one rolling into recession. The PBOC's Q2 monetary policy report pledged continued accommodative support while redirecting credit from real estate toward high-tech and green infrastructure sectors, a structural shift that is bearing measurable results in EV adoption data but has not yet translated into a Hang Seng re-rating.

Next Week: What to Watch

Jackson Hole (August 27-29) is the anchor event on the horizon, but next week delivers its own critical inputs. Chinese July retail sales and industrial production data, flagged by the PBOC itself as the key consumer response test for its stimulus pivot, land August 15. A miss there would challenge the PBOC's accommodative narrative and pressure EEM and China-exposed positions. UK labor market and wage data arrive early in the week, which will determine whether the Bank of England's rate-cut timeline accelerates or stalls after this week's GDP beat. In the U.S., retail sales and preliminary consumer sentiment readings will test whether the AI-driven equity rally has a consumer spending foundation beneath it. Watch the 30-year Treasury auction results carefully: soft dealer demand this week already pushed yields to a 25-year high, and any repeat will steepen the curve further and compress equity multiples, particularly in rate-sensitive sectors.

  • USO the Hormuz standoff escalated materially this week, with the Pentagon announcing an indefinite blockade and drone strikes hitting UAE tankers; WTI risk premium has room to expand further if the maritime situation deteriorates, making a partial energy allocation tactically justified.
  • EWJ Japan's third consecutive quarter of GDP growth at 0.6% QoQ, combined with resilient private consumption and capital expenditure, provides a macro foundation that the Nikkei's 4.51% weekly gain is reflecting accurately rather than anticipating; the yen weakness (down 0.95%) additionally supports export earnings.
  • GLD gold added 1.02% with Hormuz risk elevated, fiscal concerns driving the 30-year yield to 25-year highs, and FOMC policy uncertainty persisting into September; the fundamental case for holding gold as a portfolio anchor remains intact even without a breakout.
  • TLT reduce or avoid; the 30-year yield at 5.26% with soft primary dealer demand signals the long end is repricing the fiscal deficit independently of Fed policy, and that dynamic has no near-term resolution catalyst before the Treasury's next refunding announcement.
