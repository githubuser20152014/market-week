The Truce Expired. Gold Hit $4,624. Nobody Blinked.

Framework Foundry Weekly · Global Investor Edition

A 60-day US-Iran truce expired on August 18. Within hours, someone put a projectile into a commercial vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. Ceasefire premium: gone. Brent ripped past $92.50, the UAE cut financial ties with Tehran the next day, and the U.S. Treasury piled on secondary sanctions before the week was out.

Bonds delivered the verdict nobody wanted. The 30-year yield hit 5.335%, the highest print since June 2007, forcing the Treasury to double its long-dated buyback operations to $4 billion starting September 9, a polite way of admitting the bond market needed a babysitter. Then the July FOMC minutes leaked a hawkish faction still nostalgic for rate hikes, and crude above $90 handed them a script. September rate cuts are dead.

Gold noticed. It closed the week up 5.22% at $4,624. The Nasdaq did not, it closed down 2.26%. One of those two numbers is telling you the truth about this regime, and it isn't the one still pretending cheap money is coming back.

Here's my read:

The Fed is boxed in and everyone can see it now. July retail sales contracted 0.6% month-over-month, the sharpest consumer pullback in a year, which under normal circumstances is a green light for cutting in September. Except crude is above $87 and feeding straight into August CPI before the next FOMC meeting, and the July minutes just confirmed a hawkish faction still angling for hikes. The Fed cannot cut into oil-driven inflation and cannot hike into a consumer that's already pulling back. It's frozen, and frozen is not a policy.

The one developed-market equity index that worked this week tells you where the money actually went. The FTSE 100 gained 0.62%, the only green print among developed markets, carried by BP, Shell, and the miners riding the same commodity rally that's crushing everyone else's multiples. Jackson Hole next week, specifically Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Thursday keynote, is the one event that can flip this. A hawkish tilt reprices TLT and QQQ lower. Any softening buys equities a short breather they haven't earned.

The One Trade: Long $GLD

Gold closed at $4,624 on Friday, a 5.22% weekly gain, at the week's high with no sign of distribution. This isn't a momentum chase. Three structural catalysts converged at once and none of them are resolved: a Persian Gulf risk premium that just got repriced higher and won't revert quickly, a bond market that needed emergency Treasury intervention to function, and a dollar that fell 0.86% even as yields rose, the exact configuration that historically accelerates gold's upside for non-dollar buyers.

A Fed that can't cut, a bond market that needs a buyback to stay orderly, and a geopolitical flashpoint sitting on 20% of global oil supply are not short-term noise. They're a regime, and gold is the cleanest single trade expressing it.

Kill switch: a Strait of Hormuz diplomatic breakthrough, or a surprise dovish signal from Warsh at Jackson Hole that sends the dollar and bonds both rallying at once, would compress gold's safe-haven and inflation-hedge bids fast.

What else I'm watching

  • USO / XLE WTI is holding above $85 with tanker routing physically constrained, not just headline-scared. Ships are literally rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope. This is a structural supply story with no diplomatic off-ramp in sight, don't fade it on a quiet Tuesday.
  • EWU outperformed every developed-market equity index this week at +0.62%, and the reason, energy and mining concentration, isn't going anywhere. Stay in it against QQQ or FEZ.
  • TLT stays a short. The 30-year at 5.335% and a Treasury buyback that's a stabilizer, not a reversal, means Jackson Hole hawkishness or oil-driven CPI upside both hit duration first.
  • EWH was the only green Asia-Pacific index this week at +2.79%, riding PBOC rate stability while EWJ dropped on a brutal 4.16% Nikkei week. Treat Hong Kong as a distinct China-recovery trade, not a proxy for Japan's export-tech pain.
  • The Nikkei's 4.16% collapse is the trade nobody's talking about next to Hormuz. South Korea's KOSPI fell 5.7% the same Wednesday session, foreign investors dumped semiconductor and export names, and that regional tech unwind is still unfolding under the oil headline.
