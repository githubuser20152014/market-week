Equities fell 0.77% yesterday. Here's why you shouldn't stop at that number.

The real headline: the 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.24%, the highest sustained level since 2007. Everything else, including the Iran ceasefire rejection, is downstream of what a risk-free rate above 5% does to every other asset on the board.

Three markets are telling three different stories about what happens next.

Bonds. The 10-year at 5.24%, with swap markets now pricing a 68% probability of another hike before year-end, is not a market pricing in a pause. It's a market that thinks the Fed still has work to do, and every dollar in a richly valued equity portfolio is now competing against a genuinely attractive risk-free alternative.

Gold. Up 0.27% on a day the dollar also firmed to 101.42. That inverse relationship isn't supposed to break. When it does, it usually means institutional buyers are hedging something specific, in this case a real geopolitical flashpoint, not a generic rates trade.

Crude. Holding above $92 despite a broad risk-off tape, after the White House rejected Iran's offer to de-escalate at the Strait of Hormuz. Tanker traffic through the strait is down 85% from baseline. That isn't headline risk. That's a physical supply disruption showing up in freight data in real time.

One corporate data point worth flagging: Boeing's 737 Max 10 certification just slipped again on a software issue. Small detail, bigger signal, supply chains and certification timelines are still fragile right as the cost of capital to fix them gets more expensive by the week.

The angle nobody is covering: capital allocation is diverging globally in a way that matters more than any single day's index move. While US real yields climb and squeeze domestic growth multiples, India is readying $25 billion for deep tech investment to compete with the US and China on AI infrastructure. Capital doesn't wait for the Fed to finish its cycle. It goes where the return math still works.

So which market got the day right? Equities treated this as a normal pullback. Bonds and crude are pricing in something structural, a rate environment that isn't reverting soon, and a physical oil disruption with no resolution in sight before Thursday's OPEC+ meeting at the earliest.

I'll side with bonds and crude. They're not reacting to a headline. They're pricing a mechanism.
