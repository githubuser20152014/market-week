Nasdaq futures are up 0.74% this morning. Before you get comfortable with that number, here's why the rest of the market isn't buying it.

Three assets sat out yesterday's equity party, and all three are usually the ones that know something first.

Bonds: the 10-year yield dipped to 4.70% instead of confirming the risk-on mood. When yields fall while equities try to rally, that's not conviction, that's money hiding.

Gold: it surged to $4,687, a whisper from record highs, alongside a weakening dollar and a Treasury bond buyback program already in motion. That's not a momentum trade. That's a multi-factor hedge against Jackson Hole uncertainty, and hedges don't get built by people who think the coast is clear.

Crude: WTI cratered 2.75% to $82.67 on the back of a sweeping new Iran sanctions package, "Operation Economic Outcast," that designated more than 60 maritime entities moving Iranian crude. A sanctions package meant to squeeze supply should send oil higher. It didn't. Traders read it as a phased compliance framework, not an immediate shock, and priced in demand softening instead. When the headline says tighten and the tape says loosen, believe the tape.

The corporate world is telling a different story than the macro tape, which is exactly the tension worth watching. Lego just posted record first-half revenue, with the CEO pointing to strength at both the high end and the value end of pricing. Consumer demand is not cracking. So why is smart money quietly rotating into gold and bonds? Because markets aren't hedging the economy right now, they're hedging the Fed. Newly installed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote Friday, and his "regime change" posture has made forward guidance a thing of the past. Uncertainty about the reaction function, not the underlying economy, is what's pulling capital toward hard assets.

The angle nobody's covering: those Iran sanctions don't just hit oil, they ripple through the countries most exposed to Iranian trade lifelines, China, India, and the UAE among them. Secondary sanctions enforcement is a slower, messier process than a single headline captures, and the compliance timeline matters more than the announcement did. Watch how those trade routes reprice over the next two weeks, not just how oil traded on day one.

So which market got it right yesterday? Equities, betting Jackson Hole passes without incident and Nvidia's Wednesday print saves the week? Or gold and bonds, betting the Fed's new chair is about to remind everyone how little forward guidance is actually on offer? My money's on the hedge. Markets that bid gold and sell duration risk at the same time are rarely wrong about which way uncertainty resolves.
