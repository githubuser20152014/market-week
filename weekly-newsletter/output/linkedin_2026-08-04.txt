Wall Street decided a pause is the same thing as peace. The Dow closed at a record 53,178, the S&P jumped 1.5% to 7,600.50, and the Nasdaq ripped 2.1% higher, all because Trump paused planned Iran strikes and diplomats started talking about a Hormuz shipping corridor. Not a deal. A pause and some small talk in Muscat. Here's why I wouldn't trust the index level alone.

Bonds didn't buy it. The 10-year yield rose 8.2 basis points to 4.745% on a day equities were supposedly celebrating peace, which is not what bond markets do when they believe a risk-off catalyst has genuinely cleared. Yields climbing into an equity rally usually means the bond market is pricing something else entirely, in this case a fiscal and inflation backdrop that a diplomatic pause does nothing to fix.

Gold didn't buy it either. Gold rose 1.4% to $4,106 on the same session oil crashed and the dollar firmed, an unusual combination that typically only happens when structural buyers are hedging something bigger than a single day's headline. Three assets that normally move against gold all moved against it simultaneously, and gold still climbed.

Crude told the real story. WTI cratered 3.4% to $81.75, unwinding roughly five months of accumulated war premium in a single session on the strength of a pause, not a signed agreement. That is a lot of risk premium to remove on a maybe.

The corporate proof point: Palantir jumped 16% on what the company called "otherworldly" commercial AI revenue, strong enough that CEO Alex Karp used the earnings call to publicly accuse rival AI labs of trying to "drug addict" the world with their models. Whatever you think of the rhetoric, the underlying demand signal for enterprise AI tooling is real, and it showed up in the print, not just the multiple.

The angle nobody's covering this morning: the yen. Washington and Tokyo coordinated an intervention aimed at pulling USD/JPY back toward 155, and this morning the pair is sitting at 157.79, essentially ignoring the effort. South Korea's KOSPI dropped 3.6% overnight, the sharpest move on the Asia board, which looks a lot more like a carry-trade unwind than a reaction to Middle East headlines. If that intervention is failing to hold, it is a bigger story than a one-day equity record.

So which market got it right? Equities are pricing a durable de-escalation. Bonds and gold are pricing a fiscal and inflation problem that outlives the headline. Both can't be correct, and my money is on the bond market, because bond traders don't get paid to be optimistic, they get paid to be right.
