### Hormuz Shock Reprices Energy, Tech, and Inflation

The dominant story this week was the U.S.-Iran military confrontation, now in its twelfth consecutive night of airstrikes. Over 30 commercial vessels were targeted near the Strait of Hormuz, Houthi drones struck Saudi crude tankers in the Red Sea, and Brent crude breached $100 per barrel intraday on July 24 before settling near $98.50. **WTI closed the week at $89.31, up 6.6%**, compressing the final settlement well below the intraday spike but still marking the sharpest single-week energy move in months. The repricing is not speculative: maritime war-risk insurance premiums are rising, global shipping lines are rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, and U.S. retail gasoline crossed $4.00 per gallon.

The energy shock landed on top of two pre-existing pressures: a semiconductor rout that sent the Nasdaq down **2.9% on the week**, and the U.S. administration's formal implementation of **10-12.5% universal import tariffs across 60 trading partners** on July 24. Together, these three catalysts, an energy supply disruption, a tech valuation reset, and a structural tariff escalation, constitute a regime signal. Growth expectations are being marked down while inflation expectations are being marked up. **The 10-year Treasury yield rose 11 basis points to 4.68%**, confirming that bond markets see the oil shock as an inflation event, not a growth scare requiring Fed accommodation. The ECB, facing the same stagflationary arithmetic, held its deposit rate at 2.25% on July 23 with Christine Lagarde citing 3.0% headline inflation and deteriorating Eurozone manufacturing conditions.

The counterintuitive read: European equities outperformed this week despite Europe being a large net energy importer. The DAX gained **1.33%** and the FTSE 100 added **1.28%**. European indices benefit from a heavier weighting toward energy producers, defense contractors, and industrial exporters that price in dollars, and the leadership transition in the UK, with Andy Burnham signaling a review of North Sea energy policy, added a domestic supply angle. The divergence between US tech-heavy indices and European value-heavy indices is widening. That is a regime shift worth taking seriously.

### Equity Markets - Europe Holds Firm as US Tech Breaks Down

U.S. equities split along factor lines. The Nasdaq fell **2.9%** as institutional investors demanded clearer ROI from AI infrastructure spending: Alphabet's post-market decline after raising capex forecasts set the tone, and semiconductor indices are now down roughly 20% from recent highs. The S&P 500 lost **1.03%** and the Russell 2000 dropped **1.28%**, confirming the weakness is broad, not just a megacap issue. Small caps are particularly exposed to the tariff escalation: the new 10-12.5% import duties on 60 trading partners raise input costs for domestic manufacturers who cannot easily pass through costs. The Dow's relative outperformance, down only **0.4%**, reflects the same factor rotation seen in Europe: investors are moving toward cash-flow-positive industrials and energy names.

Europe was the clear winner. The DAX gained **1.33%**, the FTSE 100 **1.28%**, Euro Stoxx 50 **0.91%**, and the CAC 40 **0.67%**. The energy-heavy composition of European large-cap indices acted as a natural hedge against the Hormuz shock. In Asia-Pacific, the picture was mixed with a bearish tilt. The Nikkei fell **2.61%** despite Japan announcing a **¥2 trillion emergency stimulus package** on July 23, as yen weakness and tech exposure dragged the index lower. The Hang Seng gained **0.52%** but China's Q2 GDP came in at **4.3%**, missing the 4.5% consensus, with property investment down 18% in H1. MSCI EM fell **1.11%**, squeezed by dollar strength and rising oil import costs. The divergence between European value and US growth is now the clearest equity signal in the market.

### Currency Markets - Dollar Recovers as Safe-Haven Demand Returns

The USD Index closed at **101.47, up 0.65% on the week**, reversing recent dollar weakness as the Hormuz crisis triggered a familiar safe-haven bid. Every major currency lost ground against the dollar. **Sterling fell 0.93%** to 1.3319, weighed down by political uncertainty as Andy Burnham formally assumed office as the UK's sixth Prime Minister in ten years. The Swiss franc, typically the safe-haven of choice in European crises, dropped **1.03%**, the largest move in the basket, suggesting the oil shock narrative is dominant over traditional European risk-off flows. The yen fell **0.78%** despite Japan's stimulus announcement, as the Bank of Japan's upcoming policy assessment is not expected to shift materially. The euro lost **0.45%**, a contained move given the ECB hold and European equity outperformance.

For global ETF investors, dollar strength at 101.47 creates a headwind for unhedged international positions even when local-currency returns look attractive. European equity gains of 1-1.3% in local terms shrink when translated back to USD. Currency-hedged vehicles like HEDJ deserve consideration if the dollar continues to benefit from geopolitical risk premiums. Conversely, any diplomatic breakthrough in the Hormuz standoff could reverse the safe-haven dollar bid quickly, releasing upside in EM and commodity currencies.

The commodity complex as a whole is signaling an inflationary supply shock, not a demand-driven growth recovery.

### This Week's Economic Events

No formal economic data releases were tagged in the structured event feed this week. The market-moving information came from corporate earnings and policy decisions. On the earnings side, Alphabet raised its Q2 capex forecast despite strong cloud revenue growth, and the market punished the stock in after-hours trading: this is the key signal that institutional investors have shifted from rewarding AI ambition to demanding AI monetization. Tesla's elevated capex guidance added to the pressure. General Motors and 3M provided constructive industrial guidance on July 21, which contributed to the mid-week bounce. The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.25% on July 23, a widely expected decision, but Lagarde's explicit reference to stagflationary risk from energy imports was new language that signals the bar for September cuts has risen.

Japan's ¥2 trillion emergency stimulus package announced July 23 was notable for its composition: energy subsidies, small-business credit guarantees, and semiconductor manufacturing tax incentives. This is targeted counter-cyclical policy, not broad reflation. China's 4.3% Q2 GDP miss against a 4.5% consensus, with property investment down 18% in H1, confirms that the structural drag from real estate has not stabilized. The late-July Politburo meeting is now the most important upcoming policy event for EM investors.
