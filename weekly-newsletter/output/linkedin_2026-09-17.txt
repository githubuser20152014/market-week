The S&P dropped 0.45% yesterday after the Fed's first rate hike in three years. Futures are up 1.67% this morning like it never happened. Here's why you shouldn't trust the reversal.

Bonds: The 10-year Treasury closed at 5.006%, a level unseen since 2007, and hasn't moved pre-market. If a real rate hike were being calmly absorbed, yields would be doing something. They're not. Bonds are the only market in this story acting like the hike actually happened.

Gold: Gold fell nearly 1% into the close and sat flat overnight. The inflation-hedge trade, which had been running hot for weeks, quietly stood down the same week rates went up. That's not a coincidence, that's confirmation the market believes this hike sticks.

Crude: Oil dropped over 1% because Saudi Aramco rerouted crude around a Strait of Hormuz disruption via a bypass port in Oman. The geopolitical risk premium evaporated overnight, not because the underlying risk resolved, but because a logistics workaround made it temporarily irrelevant.

The corporate proof point: Salesforce issued a fiscal 2030 revenue target of over $63 billion, beating estimates, in the same week the discount rate on every future dollar of software earnings went up. Long-duration growth stories are being told with more conviction exactly when the market has less patience for them.

The angle nobody's pricing yet: a September 24 Trump-Xi summit carries proposed tariff relief on electronics and agriculture. If it lands, it's a real margin and demand shock for two sectors that have spent years pricing in permanent trade friction. If it doesn't, this morning's futures rally has even less underneath it than it looks like.

So which market got it right: the 1.67% futures pop, or the 10-year sitting at a 2007 level without blinking? The bond market has been early and correct more often than equities have this cycle. I'm siding with the desk that's boring and right, not the one that's exciting and reactive.
