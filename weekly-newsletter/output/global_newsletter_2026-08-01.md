### The FTSE Rallied 1.23% on Peace. The Russell Fell 0.45% on Everything Else.

Peace broke out and the market partied like it was an actual soft landing. It isn't one. President Trump suspended the U.S. bombing campaign against Iran on July 27, and **Brent crude cratered more than 7% to $85/bbl** in a single session, the fastest the Strait of Hormuz risk premium has evaporated all year. Cairo piled on days later with a Hamas disarmament framework, and traders treated two ceasefires like two rate cuts.

Meanwhile the actual economy did the opposite of celebrating. **U.S. Q2 GDP printed at 1.5% annualized, missing the 1.8% consensus**, core PCE sat stuck at 3.7%, and the Fed held rates for a fifth straight meeting while three of its own voting members demanded a hike anyway. Chair Warsh responded to the split by abandoning forward guidance entirely, which is Fed-speak for "we don't know either." **The 10-year Treasury yield rose 9 basis points to 4.74%** and the 30-year hit a multi-year high of 5.28%, because bond traders are pricing a policy mistake, not a Goldilocks anything.

**The counterintuitive read:** slowing growth, sticky inflation, a hawkish Fed minority, and Section 301 tariffs now blanketing 99.4% of U.S. merchandise imports is not a bull case, it's a stagflation starter kit wearing a ceasefire bow. The sugar rush has a shelf life measured in days, not quarters. Anyone treating this week's bounce as a regime shift gets run over the moment the data calendar remembers it exists.

### What it means for you

Trump suspending the bombing campaign against Iran on July 27 was the one catalyst that moved every market this week, collapsing Brent by more than 7% in a single session and setting off a relief rally that spared almost nobody. **European and Hong Kong markets were the clearest beneficiaries**, with the FTSE 100 up 1.23% and the Hang Seng surging 3.56%, while the U.S. Russell 2000 actually fell 0.45%, small caps left holding the tariff and borrowing-cost bag while the rest of the world partied. The dollar's 1.5% drop to 99.80 is a straightforward tailwind for anyone sitting in unhedged international ETFs like EWU, FEZ, or EWH. But don't confuse a ceasefire bounce with a healthy economy: **Q2 GDP came in at 1.5%, core PCE held at 3.7%**, the Fed's own vote split 9-3 with hawks demanding hikes, and the 30-year Treasury yield hit 5.28%. New Section 301 tariffs covering nearly everything the U.S. imports are a structural inflation problem, not a headline that fades by Monday. Treat this week's tech bounce as a tactical rotation, not a new bull leg, and lean into dividend-heavy international equity via EWU or FEZ while the dollar stays weak.

### The One Trade: [$EWU](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/EWU) - Long

The FTSE 100 led all developed market indices this week with a **1.23% gain**, and the structural setup argues that move has legs into next month. UK large caps have three concurrent tailwinds right now. First, the Iran ceasefire directly benefits the FTSE's heavy weighting in energy, mining, and industrial names that had been suppressed by Hormuz risk premiums for two consecutive weeks. Second, the **dollar's 1.5% weekly decline** creates a direct mechanical boost for unhedged sterling-denominated returns in dollar terms, and GBP/USD is already at 1.346. Third, UK large caps have almost no direct exposure to the USTR's 10%-12.5% Section 301 tariff structure targeting Asian and emerging market supply chains. The FTSE is a value-heavy, dividend-paying index that looks increasingly attractive relative to a U.S. market facing a GDP miss, sticky inflation, and a Fed that just produced a 9-3 hawkish split.

The trade is not without execution risk. The ECB's Kazimir flagging a potential additional hike could spill into European financial conditions broadly, and the UK remains exposed to global growth slowdown through its commodity and financial sectors. But the combination of geopolitical relief, dollar weakness, and relative tariff insulation makes UK large-cap equity the cleanest expression of this week's macro shift. The intraweek low of 10,736 held convincingly, and the FTSE closed near 10,868, well above that support.

**Confirms:** FTSE 100 holds above 10,800 into next week, and GBP/USD maintains the 1.33 handle as dollar weakness continues.
**Risk:** U.S.-Iran back-channel talks collapse or Hormuz maritime disruptions escalate, reversing the energy risk premium and hammering FTSE energy constituents.

### Equity Markets - Europe and Hong Kong Lead, Japan Stumbles

Europe won the week without lifting a finger. The **FTSE 100 gained 1.23%**, the **Euro Stoxx 50 added 1.12%**, and even the laggards, CAC 40 at +0.79% and DAX at +0.67%, still beat every U.S. benchmark, because nothing repriced European industrials and airlines faster than watching a war get called off. The ECB's Kazimir floated one more rate hike and nobody cared: an energy windfall beats a hawkish central banker every time.

Asia-Pacific turned the same ceasefire into a proper spectacle of divergence. **The Hang Seng surged 3.56%** and the ASX 200 added 2.33%, both riding the relief trade for everything it was worth. The Nikkei, unimpressed, fell **1.23%** after swinging nearly 8% top to bottom, while the KOSPI dropped a full 11% mid-week as traders decided Samsung and SK Hynix's AI capex story had gotten ahead of itself. Back home, the Dow and Nasdaq managed polite gains near 0.6% and 0.55%, but the **Russell 2000 fell 0.45%**, small caps still footing the bill for tariffs and borrowing costs the mega-caps can shrug off. Earnings did their own sorting: Amazon jumped 13% on AWS, Apple dropped 7.2% on China weakness, proof the market still knows the difference between cash flow and a story.

### Currency Markets - Dollar Breaks Down as Ceasefire Resets Risk

The **U.S. dollar index fell 1.5% to 99.80**, its worst week in months, and it broke the usual playbook doing it: yields rose while the dollar fell, a combination that isn't supposed to happen at the same time. Two safe-haven bids evaporated at once, Iran and Gaza, while the GDP miss and stubborn PCE quietly torched the "U.S. exceptionalism" trade everyone was still leaning on. The USTR's tariff blast across 60 trading partners didn't help, giving traders one more reason to wonder if dollar assets are worth the retaliatory risk.

**The yen gained 2.13%**, the euro added 1.14%, and the Swiss franc rose 1.06%, the full defensive-currency trio cashing in on relief and dollar weakness at once. For ETF investors sitting in unhedged European or Asia-Pacific exposure, that's a free mechanical boost on top of whatever the underlying index already did. Watch 99.50 on the DXY: hold it and this is a blip, break it and the rotation out of dollar assets gets a lot more serious.

### Commodities & Metals - Oil Drops 7%, Gold Softens on Ceasefire Relief

**WTI crude fell 1.68% on the week to $84.67**, a tame headline hiding a genuinely violent ride: a single-session crash of more than 7% on July 27 when the bombing campaign got suspended, taking oil to $77.78 before buyers showed up. A 2.4-million-barrel inventory draw from the EIA did the stabilizing, and Brent settled near $81.40 by July 29. Don't get comfortable: Hormuz harassment hasn't stopped, OPEC+ quota talks land in Q4, and the Senate just passed a Russia-Iran sanctions bill 86-12 aimed squarely at dark fleet tankers.

Gold slipped 1.0% to $4,049 and silver gave back 1.51%, both simply exhaling as the fear premium that pumped them left the room. Natural gas fell 2.48%. Read the commodities complex correctly: this was relief, not a demand story. The **30-year at 5.28%** is a far more durable enemy of non-yielding metals than one week of good news, and the fact gold only gave back $69 off its highs despite a full-blown ceasefire rally says the central-bank bid underneath it hasn't gone anywhere.

### This Week's Economic Events

The FOMC held rates at 3.50%-3.75% as universally expected, but **the 9-3 vote split with three dissenters pushing for an immediate hike** was the genuine surprise. Chair Warsh has now formally abandoned forward guidance, which is a deliberate choice to preserve optionality. The bond market responded by pushing 10-year yields to 4.74% and 30-year yields to a multi-year high of 5.28%, steepening the curve sharply. The macro data released Friday delivered a second punch: **Q2 GDP at 1.5% annualized missed the 1.8% consensus**, and core PCE at 3.7% year-over-year confirmed that the Fed's inflation target remains a distant destination. Taken together, the data configuration is stagflationary. The Fed cannot cut because inflation is sticky. It is reluctant to hike because growth is decelerating. The USTR's Section 301 tariff announcement, covering 10%-12.5% across 60 trading partners representing essentially all U.S. merchandise imports, adds a structural inflation input that will take quarters to fully price through supply chains.

### Next Week: What to Watch

The most important events next week are **the July nonfarm payrolls report and the ISM manufacturing index**. A weak payrolls print combined with the GDP miss would sharply accelerate September rate-cut expectations, which would rally long-duration bonds and compress the dollar further. A hot number, **anything above 180,000 with wages accelerating**, validates the hawk dissenters and sends yields higher still. The manufacturing ISM will give the first read on whether the USTR tariff shock is showing up in order books. On the geopolitical side, back-channel Oman and Geneva talks on a formal U.S.-Iran armistice continue, and international working groups convene in Cairo over the weekend to finalize Gaza verification protocols. Any breakdown in either process would reverse this week's commodity and risk relief instantly.
