Everyone is calling this week an oil shock. It's not, not really. It's a central bank credibility problem wearing an oil shock as a costume, and here's why you shouldn't trust the headline version of this story.

Yes, WTI crude surged 9.1% to $100.05 after U.S. strikes on Iranian tankers and a declared Hormuz exclusion zone. That part is real. But look at what happened underneath it, because that's where the actual signal is.

**Bonds dissented first.** The 10-year Treasury yield jumped 22 basis points to 4.97% even after Treasury Secretary Bessent deployed a $6 billion debt buyback specifically to cap it. Primary dealers took the liquidity and kept selling. When an intervention designed to hold a level fails within days, the market is telling you it doesn't believe the "transitory" framing, whatever officials are saying in public.

**Gold dissented harder.** This is the asset that is supposed to rally on exactly this kind of headline: war, oil disruption, geopolitical risk. Instead it fell 1.44% to $4,366. The explanation is real yields, not fear, are setting allocation right now. Near-5% Treasury yields make holding non-yielding bullion expensive, full stop, no matter how loud the geopolitics get.

**Crude was the only market telling the truth.** OPEC+ confirmed it is holding production quotas through October. Maritime insurers have pulled war-risk coverage for Hormuz transit. That is not sentiment, that is physical supply tightening in real time.

The corporate proof point that made this a macro story instead of a headline story: August PPI printed 5.4% year-over-year, hot against a 5.3% consensus. That inflation print, not the war, is why swap markets moved to price an 82% probability of a Fed hike at next week's FOMC meeting. Central banks were already leaning hawkish. The oil shock just removed their last excuse to wait.

Here's the angle nobody is covering: the yen carry trade unwound violently this week, with the yen jumping 1.72% against the dollar, and it dragged Asian equities down with it. The Nikkei fell 2.42%, the Hang Seng fell 3.3%, and the ASX lost 2.94%. That's not a war story. That's a leverage story that happened to have a war as its trigger, and it's still unwinding.

Meanwhile emerging markets, historically the first casualty of a dollar-yield spike plus an oil shock, closed essentially flat. China's $54 billion sovereign bond recapitalization and pre-summit optimism ahead of the September 24 Trump-Xi meeting gave EM the only positive return of the week.

So which market got it right: developed equities pricing in panic, or emerging markets shrugging it off entirely? I'll take the side that's backed by an actual policy intervention (China's recapitalization) over the side that's backed by a Treasury buyback that already failed once this week. EM read this correctly.
