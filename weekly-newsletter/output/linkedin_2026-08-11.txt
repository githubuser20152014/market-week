WTI broke through $84 yesterday. Gold sits near record highs. The S&P still closed red. Here's why you shouldn't trust either move heading into this morning's inflation print.

Bonds are the tell. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 4.699%, even as July payrolls contracted by 23,000. Weak jobs data is supposed to pull yields down. It didn't. That's the bond market saying it doesn't believe inflation is under control, no matter what the labor data says.

Gold is the second tell. Up 1.58% to $4,430.80 on a day the dollar barely moved, that's not a currency hedge. Gold doesn't climb alongside rising yields unless the market is pricing in stagflation, not just uncertainty.

Crude got its own reason entirely. The Strait of Hormuz talks between Iran and Oman collapsed, and WTI broke clean through the $78-$82 range that had held for weeks to close at $84.23. That's a supply story layered on top of two macro stories, and none of them agree with each other.

The corporate proof point: Nvidia lined up $500 billion in financing, with CEO Jensen Huang calling the company's chips an "investable asset" in their own right. That's not a company hedging against a slowdown, that's a company betting the AI capex cycle has years left to run, inflation print or not.

The angle nobody's covering: the Strategic Petroleum Reserve just fell below 300 million barrels, the lowest level since 1983. Washington built that reserve specifically to cap oil shocks. It no longer has the ammunition to do it, right as a real supply disruption is unfolding in the Gulf.

So which market has this right: bonds and gold pricing in a stagflation shock, or futures sitting barely green like everything's fine? One of them gets proven wrong by this morning's inflation print. Above 3.5% and the bond and gold trade wins. At or below 3.3% and the calm futures market wins.
