Everyone's cheering that S&P futures are up 0.6% this morning. I wouldn't trust it for a minute.

Yesterday the S&P 500 fell 0.48%, the Nasdaq dropped 0.56%, the Dow shed 0.29%. Small-caps got hit hardest, again, because small-caps are the canaries of domestic credit and this one has a persistent cough. This morning equities are bouncing. The rest of the market isn't buying it.

Here's why:

Bonds: T-note futures are down another half a point overnight, even as equity futures rally. The 10-year yield is already testing five-year highs heading into Wednesday's Fed decision. Bonds haven't believed this equity rally for months, and they're not starting now.

Gold: it pulled back on rising real yields, the textbook reaction. But BRICS de-dollarization chatter and gold-backed settlement rail proposals are putting a structural floor under it that rate math alone doesn't explain. This is a market hedging two different fears at once.

Crude: oil surged over 1.7% after Saudi Arabia's main East-West pipeline, a 1,200-kilometer artery that normally moves 5 million barrels a day, was knocked offline by drone strikes on pumping stations. Storage at the Red Sea port of Yanbu has 48 to 72 hours before it runs dry. That is not sentiment. That is a physical supply shock, and no Wednesday rate decision fixes a broken pipeline.

The proof point: oil and the 10-year yield are now moving in lockstep, something that hasn't happened in seven years. That correlation is a stagflation signal, not a growth signal, and markets have a bad track record of pricing stagflation correctly until it's too late.

The second-order story nobody's talking about: shipping insurers have been quietly pricing war-risk surcharges into Strait of Hormuz transit for weeks. Every refiner from Rotterdam to Seoul is now bidding for the same non-Gulf barrels. This isn't a one-week spike, it's a structural repricing of global crude logistics.

So which market has it right? The equity futures pricing a calm Wednesday, or the bond and commodity markets pricing a Fed cornered by an energy shock it can't hike its way out of? I'll take the bond market's word for it. Bonds have been skeptical of this rally since it started, and so far, they've been right.
