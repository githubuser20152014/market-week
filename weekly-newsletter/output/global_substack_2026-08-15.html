The Navy Blockaded Hormuz. The S&P Crossed 7,800 Like Nothing Happened.

Framework Foundry Weekly · Global Investor Edition

The U.S. Navy just launched an indefinite blockade of Iranian port traffic in the Strait of Hormuz, the choke point for roughly 20% of the planet's daily oil supply. Drone strikes hit two UAE tankers this week. The Pentagon used the word "indefinite." The S&P 500 crossed 7,800 for the first time anyway.

Underneath the record close, July CPI landed exactly on consensus: 3.4% YoY, core at 2.5%. Clean data, a cooperative Fed, a September hold locked in with three regional presidents dissenting for a hike anyway. Bonds were not fooled. The 30-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2001, closing at 5.26% on soft demand at the weekly refunding auction.

Stocks partied on the CPI print. Oil spiked on Hormuz. Long bonds did neither. Pick which one of those three is actually pricing the news correctly.

Here's my read:

Two stories ran in parallel this week and only one made the front page. Story one: July CPI and PPI both came in cooperative, CPI at 3.4% YoY, PPI flat at 0.0% MoM with the annual rate decelerating from 5.5% to 4.7%. That's about as clean a disinflation print as the Fed could ask for, and it's why a September hold is now the base case despite three FOMC hawks dissenting for a hike in July.

Story two: the fiscal side of the bond market did not care. The 30-year yield closed at 5.26%, its highest since 2001, on soft primary dealer demand at the refunding auction. Disinflation solves the Fed's problem. It does not solve the deficit's problem. The yield curve is steepening for the worst possible reason: not because growth is accelerating, but because the debt stock is repricing.

Layer in Hormuz. The Navy redirected 59 commercial vessels, disabled three, boarded two, and the Pentagon confirmed an indefinite blockade of Iranian port traffic after drone strikes hit two UAE-flagged tankers. WTI crude surged 5.1% to $82.40 even after absorbing a 17.4 million barrel inventory build mid-week. Hormuz handles a fifth of the world's daily oil shipments. If the standoff holds, the inflation-convergence story the Fed is banking on for September runs straight into a supply shock before Jackson Hole even opens.

The One Trade: Long $IWM

The Russell 2000 gained 1.39% this week, outrunning the S&P by nearly a full point, and rate-sensitive small caps are exactly where a cooperative CPI print should show up first. Small-cap balance sheets carry proportionally more floating-rate debt than large caps, so every basis point of hike risk that gets priced out is a direct hit to their interest expense line. Cooperative CPI plus flat PPI did precisely that this week, and the FOMC's own 9-3 vote to hold, despite three hawkish dissents, means the risk that started this trade is now the known tail, not the base case.

Hedge funds rebuilt long U.S. equity exposure into Jackson Hole this week, and small caps are the highest-beta way to express that positioning in a VIX-14 environment that is actively rewarding investors for leaning in.

Kill switch: July PCE core comes in above 2.7%, reviving the three hawkish FOMC dissenters and pushing September hike odds back above 60%, which erases the rate-sensitivity tailwind fast.

What else I'm watching

  • USO the Hormuz blockade isn't priced for an escalation, it's priced for a headline that fades. If the maritime standoff deteriorates further, WTI's risk premium has real room to expand. A partial energy allocation here is tactically justified, not greedy.
  • EWJ Japan just posted its third straight quarter of GDP growth, 0.6% QoQ, and the Nikkei's 4.51% jump is catching up to fundamentals rather than running ahead of them. Yen weakness on top of that is a straight tailwind for exporter earnings. Don't you dare sell into this.
  • GLD gold only added 1.02% to $4,380 despite a 25-year high on the long bond and an active Hormuz risk premium, a quiet week by gold's recent standards. Don't mistake quiet for done, the fundamental case, fiscal repricing plus geopolitical risk, hasn't gone anywhere.
  • TLT stays a stay-away. The 30-year at 5.26% is repricing fiscal risk independently of anything the Fed does at the short end, and there's no catalyst before the next refunding announcement to reverse that.
  • The Hang Seng's 2.67% drop is the trade nobody's talking about. Typhoon Dolphin disruptions are the excuse, but the real story is Beijing's WTO dispute filing against U.S. industrial tariffs sitting underneath it, unresolved and getting less attention than a blockade in the Persian Gulf.
