Gridlock was supposed to be bullish. The S&P 500 lost 3.1% this week anyway.

Democrats swept Congress, the Dow cheered for exactly one session, and then Thursday arrived with the Nasdaq down 5.5% in a day. Here is why you should not trust the dip-buying that followed.

The markets that refused to play along tell the real story.

Bonds: the 10-year Treasury yield sat at 4.86% on Friday after the October jobs report printed 165,000 against roughly 60,000 expected. A hot labor market, a Fed with December hike odds rising, and a stock market that wants rate cuts. Those three things do not fit in the same room.

Gold: it cleared $4,000 an ounce mid-week and closed near $4,066, up about 4.5% on the week. It held through a jobs-driven yield spike and a firmer dollar. An asset that shrugs off the usual headwinds is not trading on mood. It is trading on a view about fiscal gridlock and European stagflation.

Crude: WTI started the week above $100 and fell 11.3% to $90.83 as Qatari mediators floated a Hormuz transit deal and Trump admitted energy inflation cost Republicans Congress. Nothing lowers oil prices like losing an election.

The corporate proof point: SK hynix dropped more than 10% and Samsung fell over 6% on Friday as the AI chip unwind hit Asia. Korea's Kospi fell about 6% in a single session.

The second-order story is the regional split nobody is pricing. The Nikkei lost 10.7% on the week and entered a bear market. The Hang Seng gained 5.1% because Beijing leaned on banks and brokerages for market support. Europe held up, with the CAC 40 up 2.3%, while eurozone inflation hit 4.0% and the Bank of England hiked to 4.0%.

So which market got it right? Equities say the AI trade is a buying opportunity. Gold, oil and bonds say the political cycle just made the whole thing harder to underwrite. Gold and bonds have the better record in a regime change. One of these groups is wrong, and I know which one I would bet against.
