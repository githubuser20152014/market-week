Oil rose 3% Friday to $94.25 (markets were shut Monday for Labor Day) while the Strait of Hormuz, which moves more than 20% of the world's daily crude supply, saw commercial transit collapse to as few as two ships a window.

Here's why you shouldn't trust the 3%: nobody else confirmed the "contained" story.

Bonds didn't buy it. The 10-year yield climbed to 4.784% even as the S&P sold off 0.38% and the Dow shed 0.51%. Rising yields on a down day for stocks isn't a flight-to-safety pattern, it's a market pricing inflation risk from crude spilling into everything else.

Gold didn't buy it either. It added 0.26% to $4,441 and held that level straight through a risk-off session. When the safe-haven asset doesn't sell off on a bad day for equities, that's not indecision, that's a position.

The yen didn't buy it. It surged through 154, forcing carry-trade unwinds that spilled into leveraged tech and crypto books overnight. Currency markets tend to move first and explain themselves later.

The corporate proof point that shows markets can still move fast when they want to: Novartis fell nearly 10%, its worst day on record, on three separate drug trial setbacks in a single session. That's what real repricing looks like. Crude's 3% move, by comparison, looks slow.

The angle nobody's covering: marine war-risk insurance. Underwriters are refusing to write coverage for unescorted tankers through Hormuz. Nobody needs to plant a mine to shut a chokepoint, they just need an insurer to say no. That's a quieter, slower blockade, and it doesn't show up in the daily oil print until it does.

So which market got it right on Friday? Oil's 3% saying this is contained, or bonds, gold, and the yen all saying it isn't? Three dissents against one calm print isn't a coincidence, it's a signal that crude hasn't finished catching up to the risk.
