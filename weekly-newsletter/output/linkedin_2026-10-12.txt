Houthi missiles hit two Saudi airports this weekend. Gold fell.

Read that again. The oldest safe haven on earth sold off during a war, and US stocks still closed green. Here is why you shouldn't trust the calm.

Three markets are not buying the story.

Bonds. The 10-year yield rose to 5.244%. The equity rally is being priced at a borrowing cost that makes every future dollar of earnings worth less. Bonds never bought it, and they are not pretending to.

Gold. It dropped 0.44% to $4,198 while missiles flew. That is not gold failing. That is the dollar collecting the flight-to-safety bid instead, with the USD Index at 102.44 and USD/JPY at 158.63. The haven trade did not disappear. It changed addresses.

Crude. WTI jumped 1.09% to $92.85 and Brent is above $105. Oil was already sitting near $90 on Strait of Hormuz warnings. The airport strikes did not create the premium. They renewed its lease.

The corporate tell: the CEOs of Intel and Broadcom are now warning that a capital crunch could become AI's biggest bottleneck. At a 5%+ risk-free rate, the most expensive story in the market has a funding problem. Nasdaq futures are down 0.23% while S&P futures sit flat. Someone is quietly leaving tech.

The second-order story nobody is covering: the supply chain. Oil at these levels is a tax on everything that moves, and global inventory buffers are already thin. A premium that lives in crude eventually shows up in freight, in food, and in the grocery bill. Headline inflation is not done with you.

So which market got it right? Stocks say all clear. Bonds, gold, and oil say otherwise. Stocks are the only one of the four that needs the other three to be wrong. I'm siding with the three.

JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, and Citigroup report before the open today. High rates should flatter bank income. They will not fix a war. If the banks beat and oil keeps climbing, you get the strangest combination in markets: great earnings and a worse macro backdrop, on the same morning.
