Two ceasefires broke out this week and equities partied like it was a soft landing. It wasn't. Q2 GDP missed consensus by 30 basis points, the Fed's own vote split 9-3, and long bond yields hit multi-year highs on the exact same week stocks were celebrating peace. Here's why the bounce doesn't survive contact with next week's data.

**The dissenting markets:**

Bonds weren't fooled. The 10-year yield rose 9 basis points to 4.74% and the 30-year hit a multi-year high of 5.28%, the sharpest pushback the rates market has given a "risk-on" week in months. Bond traders don't rally on ceasefires, they rally on growth and inflation data, and this week's growth and inflation data was bad.

Gold barely moved. It slipped just 1% to $4,049, giving back only $69 off its highs despite a full-blown geopolitical relief rally that should have hammered a safe-haven asset. When gold refuses to sell off into good news, the market underneath is telling you something the headlines aren't.

Crude did move, and that matters too. WTI fell more than 7% in a single session when the bombing campaign was suspended, but it found a floor near $78 and clawed back to $84.67 by the weekend, well above the panic low. That floor is the tell: OPEC+ quota talks loom in Q4 and the Strait of Hormuz risk hasn't disappeared, it's just off the front page for now.

**The corporate proof point:**

Amazon jumped 13% on AWS strength. Apple dropped 7.2% on China revenue weakness. Same week, same tape, opposite verdicts, because the market is done rewarding growth stories without cash flow behind them. That's the real signal buried under the ceasefire noise: capital is getting more selective, not more generous.

**The second-order story nobody's covering:**

South Korea's KOSPI collapsed 11% mid-week as traders dumped Samsung and SK Hynix on AI capex skepticism. If that skepticism crosses the Pacific and hits U.S. semiconductor names the way it just hit Korea's, the AI trade that's carried this entire market for two years gets its first real stress test, and it won't be a ceasefire headline that triggers it.

**The verdict:**

So which market got the week right: the FTSE, up 1.23% on relief and dollar weakness, or the Russell 2000, down 0.45% while absorbing the actual macro data? The FTSE is trading the headline. The Russell is trading the balance sheet. One of them is going to be very wrong by September, and it's not the one pricing in tariffs and a 5.28% long bond.
