Iran fired ballistic missiles at U.S. forces overnight. The S&P is up small this morning. If your takeaway from those two sentences is "markets shrugged it off," you're reading the wrong tape.

Here's why you shouldn't trust the calm.

Bonds didn't buy it. The 10-year yield fell on the news, a genuine flight-to-safety move, not a rounding error. When Treasuries rally on a geopolitical headline while equities barely blink, one of those markets is mispricing the risk. Bonds have a better track record this cycle.

Gold didn't buy it either. It's holding near its highs even after a stretch of broadly risk-on sentiment in equities, which means the metal is still pricing in something stocks haven't fully absorbed. Gold doesn't rally for the fun of it. It rallies because someone, somewhere, is hedging a scenario the equity market is choosing to ignore.

Crude is the one market that reacted like the headline actually mattered. A geopolitical risk premium got priced back into oil overnight, the kind of move that doesn't happen on noise. Crude is telling you this isn't over. Equities are telling you it already is. They can't both be right.

Big Tech's latest earnings didn't help the case for complacency. Growth names took selling pressure after reporting, and semiconductor names in Asia sold off hard on AI-capex-ROI doubts before the missile strike even landed. The AI trade was already nervous. Geopolitics just gave it a reason to say so out loud.

Here's the angle nobody's covering: defense budgets are about to move, and not because of a headline cycle. Missile production commitments were already in motion before last night's strike. That's not a one-day trade reacting to news. That's a multi-year reallocation hiding inside a 24-hour story.

So which market got it right: bonds and gold pricing real risk, or equities pricing a shrug? History says fade the shrug. The bond market has been early to nearly every genuine risk-off move this cycle, and it just moved again.
