Every market got the same headline yesterday: progress toward reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Only one of them didn't believe it.

Oil dropped 5.7% in a single session. Stocks hit records. And gold, which had every reason to sell off on a de-escalation day, added 1.5% instead.

Here's why you shouldn't just trust the record close and move on:

Bonds: the 10-year yield fell 5.9 basis points on the same day the S&P hit an all-time high. That's a genuine bond-equity confirmation, not the usual tug of war. Lower oil takes the CPI sting out of the next print, and bond buyers moved on it immediately.

Crude: WTI settled down 5.7% at $75.77 after Treasury Secretary Bessent said a deal to reopen the Strait could land within days. That's the market pricing out a risk premium that had been embedded in oil for months, in a single trading session.

Gold: the outlier. It should have been the biggest loser on a peace headline, geopolitical hedges usually get sold first. Instead it ground higher 1.5%. Small move on the surface, wrong direction for the narrative everyone else traded. It points to a buyer who isn't hedging Iran. They're hedging something structural, the dollar, the fiscal backdrop, or both.

The proof point: Palantir's earnings did the heavy lifting on the equity side, shares ripped nearly 30% and pulled software names higher across the board. But the more useful data point came after hours. SpaceX, in its first earnings report since going public, beat on revenue and narrowed its loss, then dropped 8% anyway because capital expenditure hit $18.4 billion in a single quarter, mostly AI infrastructure. Strong results plus spending fear equals a sell. That's the entire AI capex debate in one earnings report.

The angle nobody's pricing yet: Beijing just closed the offshore trust loophole. A new 20% levy applies retroactively to assets moved into offshore trusts since 2023, and wealthy Chinese families have a 90-day window to declare and pay. Lawyers in Hong Kong and Singapore are already fielding a rush of calls, and the expectation is that families will liquidate listed Hong Kong and A-share holdings, the most liquid assets in most of these portfolios, to cover the bill. That's a forced-selling story building quietly under the surface of every other headline this week.

So which market has it right, the one betting on peace or the one that isn't buying it? Oil and equities are underwriting a deal that hasn't been signed. Gold is underwriting the idea that whatever gets signed doesn't fix the dollar. One of them is going to be wrong by Friday.
