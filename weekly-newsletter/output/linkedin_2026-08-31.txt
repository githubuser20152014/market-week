Equities fell modestly Monday and every headline called it a "shrug." I don't buy it, and neither did bonds, gold, or oil. Here's why the equity tape is the market you should trust the least this morning.

THE DISSENTING MARKETS

Bonds: the 10-year Treasury yield jumped 4.8 basis points to 4.72% on the same day equities sold off. That's backwards. In a genuine flight to safety, bonds rally and yields fall. Instead yields rose, because Fed Chair Kevin Warsh used his Jackson Hole debut to confirm rate cuts are off the table, with swap markets now pricing a 57% chance of a September hike. This isn't fear. It's a policy repricing.

Gold: up 0.5% to $4,500.80, holding firm even as the dollar softened and equities fell at the same time. Gold rising alongside both dollar weakness and equity weakness is a double signal worth taking seriously. It usually means the market doesn't trust the "nothing to see here" story either.

Oil: WTI crude spiked 3.7% to $86.52 after U.S. Central Command struck Iranian rocket positions on Larak Island, following Iran's deployment of naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, the corridor roughly 20% of global oil transit runs through. This is not a scheduled data print moving markets. It's a live, unresolved geopolitical event.

THE PROOF POINT

The clearest evidence sits in the water, not a balance sheet: a commercial tanker took two mine hits in that corridor over the weekend. That's not a hypothetical risk premium anymore. It's a physical, insured loss event, and Gulf maritime war-risk insurance will reprice accordingly, which is a cost that eventually lands on every barrel and container moving through the strait.

THE ANGLE NOBODY'S COVERING

Freight and maritime insurance. Every extra day the Hormuz corridor stays contested adds cost to global shipping lanes long before it ever shows up in a CPI print. The oil headline gets the attention. The freight and logistics story is quieter and arguably stickier.

THE VERDICT

So which market has it right: the equity tape that fell less than half a percent, or the bond, gold, and oil markets that are all pricing real stress? I'll take the three markets that don't sell optimism for a living.
