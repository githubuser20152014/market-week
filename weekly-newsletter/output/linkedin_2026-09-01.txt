Stocks fell 0.58% Tuesday on a hawkish Fed and a live war in the Strait of Hormuz. The easy read: risk-off, geopolitics wins, buy the fear later. Here's why you shouldn't trust that headline.

Look at what actually moved instead of what's making headlines.

Bonds: The 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 4.758% (up 8.6bps) even as equities sold off. That's not classic risk-off behavior, in a real flight to safety bonds rally and yields fall. The bond market is pricing a Fed hike ahead of a war premium. Rates, not geopolitics, are driving this tape.

Gold: Down 0.20% to $4,422 during a live shooting war over 20% of the world's oil supply. Gold is the asset that's supposed to love exactly this scenario and it shrugged. When the safe haven doesn't show up to its own crisis, that tells you the crisis isn't what's actually pricing the market. The dollar and yields are.

Crude: WTI jumped 2.34% to $87.77 as Hormuz throughput fell over 40%. This is the one market behaving exactly as the headlines suggest, because it's the only one directly exposed to the physical disruption. Everything else is noise around a rate story.

Venezuela quietly granted a U.S.-backed energy firm 100-year concessions on 17 oil fields this week, the kind of long-horizon capital commitment that only makes sense if you expect structurally higher, stickier energy prices for decades, not a one-week Hormuz spike. That's the real tell on where energy capital thinks this is heading.

The angle nobody's covering: Hormuz isn't just an oil chokepoint, it's a shipping chokepoint. Tankers rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope add weeks of transit time and hundreds of dollars a barrel in freight costs, and that same corridor carries LNG and dry bulk grain shipments that don't make the oil headlines. A prolonged closure doesn't just move crude, it moves global food and fertilizer freight costs right as harvest logistics are locking in. Watch ag and shipping quietly, not just energy.

So which market got it right Tuesday: bonds pricing a hawkish Fed, gold refusing to panic, or crude pricing a live supply shock? They can't all be right at once. My read: bonds and gold have this correct. Crude's move is real but temporary, the rate story is the one that sticks past this week.
