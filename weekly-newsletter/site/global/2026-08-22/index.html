The Truce Expired. Gold Hit $4,624. Nobody Blinked.

Nobody needed a data release this week. A 60-day US-Iran truce expired on August 18, and within hours somebody put a projectile into a commercial vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. Ceasefire premium: gone. Brent ripped past $92.50. The UAE cut financial ties with Tehran on the 19th. The Treasury piled on secondary sanctions the same day. Tanker traffic through the strait fell to three-month lows by the 20th. This is a supply shock wearing a geopolitics costume, and everyone is pricing it as one.

Bonds delivered the verdict nobody wanted to hear. The 30-year yield hit 5.335%, the highest print since June 2007, forcing the Treasury to double its long-dated buyback operations to $4 billion starting September 9, which is a polite way of saying the bond market needed a babysitter. That bought a few days of calm. Then the July FOMC minutes leaked a hawkish faction still nostalgic for rate hikes, and crude above $90 just handed them a script. September rate cuts are dead. Energy-driven inflation is back from the grave, long yields are repricing on the fly, and every equity multiple built on cheap money is about to find out what a margin call feels like.

What This Means For You

The expiration of the US-Iran diplomatic truce on August 18 and the immediate tanker strike in the Strait of Hormuz drove the week's biggest moves: WTI crude surged 5.49% to $87 and gold jumped 5.22% to $4,624, both closing at or near weekly highs. US equities paid the price, with the Nasdaq down 2.26% and the S&P 500 off 1.49%, as the 30-year Treasury yield hit a 19-year high of 5.335% before the Treasury doubled its bond-buyback operations to $4 billion per operation starting September 9. The Fed is now trapped: July retail sales contracted 0.6% month-over-month, the sharpest drop in a year, but oil-driven inflation and a hawkish FOMC faction make September rate cuts nearly impossible. The FTSE 100, up 0.62% via EWU, was the only developed-market equity index to gain, carried by energy and mining heavyweights directly benefiting from the commodity rally. Jackson Hole next week, specifically Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's keynote, is the single most important event for positioning: a hawkish tilt reprices TLT and QQQ lower, while any sign of softening gives equities a short-term reprieve.

The One Trade

Gold closed at $4,624 on Friday, a 5.22% weekly gain, closing at the week's high with no sign of distribution. The setup is not a momentum chase. Three structural catalysts converged simultaneously this week and none of them are resolved. First, the US-Iran truce expired without renewal, a tanker was struck in the Strait of Hormuz, the UAE severed financial ties with Tehran, and the USS George Washington carrier group entered CENTCOM waters. Persian Gulf risk premiums are repriced higher and will not revert quickly. Second, the 30-year Treasury yield hit a 19-year high of 5.335%, the US Treasury was forced to intervene with emergency buybacks, and the July FOMC minutes revealed internal pressure to resume hiking. Sovereign debt dysfunction is a direct gold catalyst. Third, the dollar fell 0.86% despite rising yields, a configuration that historically accelerates gold's upside because it removes the currency headwind for non-dollar buyers.

The macro regime here is genuinely gold-favorable, not just tactically. A Fed that cannot cut because of oil-driven inflation, a bond market requiring active Treasury intervention to prevent structural dislocation, and an active geopolitical flashpoint controlling 20% of global petroleum supply are not short-term noise. They are regime-level conditions. The FTSE 100's outperformance this week, driven by energy and mining heavyweights, confirms that commodity-linked assets are the place to be when equities are selling off and bonds are also selling off. Gold is the cleanest single expression of that regime.

Confirms: Gold holds above $4,500 on any intraweek pullback and the 10-year yield remains above 4.65%, confirming the stagflationary regime that supports the trade. Risk: A Strait of Hormuz diplomatic breakthrough or a surprise dovish signal from Warsh at Jackson Hole that drives a sharp dollar rally and bond-market relief, compressing gold's safe-haven and inflation-hedge bids simultaneously.

Equity Markets

US equities had the week everyone saw coming and nobody hedged for. The Nasdaq fell 2.26% and the S&P 500 dropped 1.49%, with Wednesday doing most of the damage as Nvidia, Broadcom, and Micron got dragged lower while the 10-year flirted with 4.75%. The Russell 2000 shed 1.39%, proof the pain was not a megacap-only problem: small caps just have less room to hide from rising borrowing costs. A Thursday bounce, courtesy of the Treasury's buyback announcement and Walmart and Home Depot posting less-terrible-than-feared earnings, clawed back some ground. Indices still finished well off their August highs, because one good afternoon does not undo a supply shock.

Europe split down the middle, literally. The FTSE 100 gained 0.62%, the only green developed-market index all week, riding BP, Shell, and the miners straight up the commodity rally. Everyone else got the memo late: the CAC 40 fell 1.67%, the DAX dropped 1.34%, and the Euro Stoxx 50 lost 1.31% as Bund yields climbed and energy input costs did what energy input costs do. Asia had the worst week of anyone. The Nikkei 225 collapsed 4.16%, anchored by a Wednesday session where it fell 3.2% in a day as foreign investors dumped semiconductor and export names like they were on fire. South Korea's KOSPI dropped 5.7% the same day, because when Tokyo sneezes, Seoul catches the flu. The Hang Seng was the lone bright spot, gaining 2.79%, on PBOC rate stability and bargain hunters circling Chinese tech that had already been beaten to a pulp.

Currency Markets

The US Dollar Index fell 0.86% to 98.80, which makes no sense until you remember nothing about this week made sense. Hormuz escalation plus rising yields is supposed to be a dollar-bull combo. Instead, the Treasury's buyback intervention read as a policy put on long bonds, and dollar paper got a little less special while commodity and European currencies rode the raw-materials rally. The Swiss franc was the week's standout, gaining 1.46% to 1.2487, doing its one job: being the currency people flee to when the Gulf gets scary. The euro gained 0.89% to 1.1678 and sterling added 0.73%, both winning by default rather than merit, dollar weakness dressed up as European strength. The yen managed a modest 0.19%, held back by Japan's Q2 GDP miss of 0.3% quarterly against 0.5% expected, which took the BOJ's hiking ambitions off the table for now. For globally diversified ETF investors, a soft dollar is a free tailwind on unhedged international exposure like EFA, EWU, and EWH. The only real question left: does the dollar stay soft into Jackson Hole, or does Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's keynote remind everyone the Fed still runs this show and send DXY back above 100?

Commodities & Metals

Gold surged 5.22% to $4,624, closing at its week high because every gold-bullish variable decided to show up to the same party: geopolitical shock, a soft dollar, real-yield confusion, and a Treasury market that needed an intervention. This is not a crowded momentum trade chasing itself. Central bank reserve managers, macro funds de-risking equities, and retail inflation hedgers all hit the bid at the same time, which is the kind of coincidence that is not one. Silver outdid it, gaining 6.47% to $69.47, industrial demand piling onto an already-scorching precious metals trade.

WTI crude gained 5.49% to $87.06, spending most of the back half of the week comfortably above $85 while Brent cleared $92.50 by Friday. The cause is not a mystery: the US-Iran truce expired August 18, a tanker got hit in the Strait of Hormuz that same day, the UAE cut financial ties with Tehran on the 19th, and tanker throughput through the strait hit three-month lows by the 20th. Nobody is speculating here. Ships are rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, war-risk insurance got repriced upward overnight, and the USS George Washington carrier group showed up in CENTCOM waters just to make the point official. Natural gas added 2.97%, riding crude's coattails while Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refining capacity quietly chipped away at regional supply confidence heading into autumn.

This Week’s Economic Events

The week's most consequential data release arrived Monday: US July retail sales contracted 0.6% month-over-month, the sharpest consumer pullback in over a year. That print alone would have been enough to anchor September rate-cut expectations. But it was immediately overwhelmed by the energy shock. Higher crude prices will feed directly into August CPI, which lands before the September FOMC meeting, and the July FOMC minutes confirmed that at least one internal Fed faction would push for renewed rate hikes if disinflation reverses. The net result: the Fed is frozen. It cannot cut into an oil-driven inflation reacceleration, and it cannot hike into a consumer that is already pulling back. Japan's Q2 GDP missed at 0.3% quarterly versus 0.5% expected, driven by a 1.2% decline in corporate capital investment. That softness takes BOJ normalization off the near-term agenda. China's July industrial output came in at 4.5% year-on-year, down from 5.3% in June and below the 4.8% forecast, while retail sales grew only 0.6% against a 1.5% expectation. The PBOC held its one-year and five-year LPR unchanged at 3.00% and 3.50% for the 15th consecutive month, choosing targeted liquidity tools over broad cuts.

Next Week: What to Watch

Jackson Hole owns the calendar. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh speaks Thursday, at a symposium themed "Financial Innovation," a suspiciously calm title for a week this unhinged. With the July FOMC minutes exposing a live rate-hike debate and WTI sitting above $87, any hawkish word choice from Warsh gets read as gospel and duration assets pay for it immediately. Watch the 10-year: a decisive break above 4.80% puts TLT and QQQ back in the crosshairs. Preliminary August manufacturing PMIs out of the US and Europe land early in the week and will be the first honest read on whether the energy shock is already denting activity. Any further Hormuz escalation, a second vessel strike or an Iranian move against a US-escorted convoy, sends Brent through $95 and forces every commodity desk to redo its homework. GCC emergency meetings in Muscat and sanctions reviews in Abu Dhabi and London also run early in the week, and any further hardening between the UAE and Iran matters directly for routing and insurance spreads.

Global Investor Positioning
  • GLD remains the highest-conviction hold: gold closed at a weekly high with no distribution, supported by Persian Gulf supply shock, 19-year Treasury yield highs, and dollar weakness, all three conditions intact entering next week.
  • USO / XLE offer direct crude exposure as WTI holds above $85 with physical tanker routing genuinely constrained; the Hormuz standoff is not a headline risk, it is a structural supply disruption with no near-term diplomatic off-ramp visible.
  • EWU outperformed every developed-market equity index this week at +0.62% and the structural reason, energy and mining sector concentration, is not going away; consider adding or holding as a relative value position against QQQ or FEZ.
  • TLT remains a tactical short or underweight: the 30-year yield hit 5.335% and the Treasury buyback intervention is a stabilizer, not a reversal catalyst; Jackson Hole hawkishness and oil-driven CPI upside both threaten duration.
  • EWH was the only Asia-Pacific index in the green at +2.79% and the PBOC's rate hold provides a stable borrowing backdrop; position selectively here as a China recovery trade distinct from the export-tech exposure in EWJ, which dropped 4.16% on Nikkei weakness.
